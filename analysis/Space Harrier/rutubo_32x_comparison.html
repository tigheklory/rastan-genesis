The Rutubo Method

Two Super Scaler arcade legends, two 2 MB 32X carts, one consistent engineering playbook. What the byte evidence says Rutubo did to bring Space Harrier and After Burner home — and the single place their approaches diverge.

01

The shared playbook

Both teardowns show the same five decisions. This is the signature of a studio that had solved the "arcade Super Scaler → 32X" problem once and applied the solution again.

01

Keep the data, rewrite the code

Arcade game data (stages, enemies, patterns, math tables) is hardware-independent and expensive to recreate, so it's copied in. The arcade executable engine is tied to dead silicon, so it's thrown away and re-authored.

02

Boot like any 32X game

Both carts start at the stock Sega "MARS" 32X security/init stub and run Rutubo's own Genesis-68000 + SH-2 program. Neither boots into arcade code.

03

Put the Super Scaler on the SH-2s

The arcade sprite-scaling hardware has no 32X equivalent, so its function — drawing cels at arbitrary zoom — is reimplemented as SH-2 code writing into the 32X framebuffer.

04

Reuse the Z80 sound

The arcade sound CPU is a Z80 and so is the Genesis's, so the sound driver and sequence data carry over substantially — ~60% for Space Harrier, ~49% for After Burner.

05

Re-encode everything format-bound

Zoomable sprite cels, background tiles, road/ground patterns, PCM samples and zoom lookup tables are all stored in arcade-specific bit layouts with no meaning on the 32X. In both games, every one of these is absent from the cart and rebuilt for the SH-2 renderer, the Genesis VDP and the Genesis sound path.

03

Where they diverge

The playbook is identical; what counted as "data worth keeping" is not, and the reason is the arcade hardware each game ran on.

Space Harrier

flat checkerboard ground · simple sub-CPU

The ground is a scrolling checkerboard — cheap to draw and cheap to recompute. Its second 68000 had little baked math worth salvaging, so it was dropped almost entirely. The prize lift was the main-CPU stage/enemy data — one 64 KB bank copied byte-for-byte.

After Burner II

true 3D projection · heavy sub-CPU

The jet, missiles and banking horizon are projected and scaled in real 3D every frame, driven by large baked tables in the sub-CPU ROM. Recreating those exactly is painful, so Rutubo copied 94% of them — and the SH-2 scaler renders using the arcade's own numbers.

Put simply: Space Harrier kept the game's content; After Burner kept the game's content and the arcade's 3D mathematics. The harder the arcade's custom silicon worked, the more of its baked data survived into the 32X — because that data is exactly what you don't want to reverse-engineer and retype by hand.

04

What this tells us about Rutubo

The rumor that these carts "run the arcade 68000 code on the Genesis" is, in both cases, false in the literal sense and revealing in the useful sense. No arcade engine executes on the 32X. But Rutubo wasn't writing clean-room reimplementations either — they were performing a disciplined hardware translation: isolate the portable payload (data, tables, Z80 sound), lift it intact, and rebuild only the parts bonded to the dead arcade silicon (the engine, the Super Scaler, the asset formats).

It's the same philosophy a faithful modern port follows: preserve the authoritative arcade data and intent, replace the arcade-chip-specific execution with native hardware realization. Rutubo just did it in 1994, by hand, twice — and the byte-for-byte fingerprints are still sitting in the carts.

About this comparison

Companion to the two detailed teardowns (space_harrier_32x_rom_teardown.html and after_burner_32x_rom_teardown.html) in the same folder. All figures are measured by de-interleaving each 2 MB 32X image into even/odd streams and content-matching every arcade EPROM in 32-byte high-entropy windows, with blocks confirmed by exact contiguous-run extension and 68000 disassembly. Code-vs-data separation within each program is by disassembly and per-region coverage. Arcade ROM roles are from MAME (segahang.cpp / segaxbd.cpp). Original ROM files were treated as read-only; all analysis ran on copies.
